# Summary

Anyons are particle-like excitations that can occur in two-dimensional quantum systems. Unlike ordinary particles, exchanging certain anyons can change a collective quantum state, a property that makes them candidates for topological quantum computing. Calculations with anyons depend on *fusion rules*, which describe how their charges combine, and on numerical *F* and *R* symbols, which describe changes of fusion basis and exchanges. These coefficients must satisfy stringent identities: otherwise, two equivalent sequences of operations can give different answers.

**Bradiant** is an open-source Python library for inspecting such data before they enter a simulation or theoretical calculation. Given finite multiplicity-free fusion rules and explicit complex F- and R-symbol tables, it tests fusion-data completeness, tensor-unit conventions, unitarity, pentagon and hexagon coherence, and braid-group relations. It also constructs braid matrices in specified fusion-path bases and computes Frobenius--Perron dimensions, double-braiding phases, and transparent-object diagnostics. Reference models, a documented JSON format, command-line and Python interfaces, and deterministic machine-readable reports make the calculations reproducible. The package evaluates supplied models; it does not derive category data or simulate physical hardware.

# Statement of need

Topological quantum computation describes information encoded nonlocally in collective states of quasiparticles, with gates generated by exchanges [@nayak2008; @pachos2012]. Its mathematical input includes fusion and braiding data. An apparently reasonable coefficient table can nevertheless be inconsistent because of missing channels, incompatible gauge conventions, or an incorrect basis transformation. Merely checking that an exchange matrix is unitary is insufficient: its associators and exchanges must also satisfy coherence identities across the allowed charge sectors.

Bradiant supplies a verification step between an external source of category data and a downstream calculation. For each admissible sector, it compares two paths through the pentagon or hexagon coherence diagram as complex matrices. A representative reported residual is

$$
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{pent}}=
\max_{s}\,\max_{i,j}\left|\bigl[P_s^{(1)}-P_s^{(2)}\bigr]_{ij}\right|,
$$

where $s$ ranges over admissible charge sectors and $P_s^{(1)}$ and $P_s^{(2)}$ are the corresponding composite recoupling matrices. Reporting both the global maximum and the failing sector makes numerical errors traceable. The intended users are researchers working with braided fusion categories, anyonic algorithms, and computational models of topological phases, as well as developers who exchange F/R datasets between software packages.

# State of the field

Several existing programs address related but different problems. **Anyonica** is used for symbolic construction and analysis of fusion-category data, including systematic classification of low-rank multiplicity-free categories [@vercleyen2026]. **TensorCategories.jl** is a broader Julia framework for computational category theory, supporting skeletal fusion data and related algebraic operations [@tensorcategories2026]. **TQSim** implements an anyon-based topological circuit simulator [@tqsim]. These projects have capabilities beyond the scope of Bradiant; no claim of priority for coherence checking or braid-matrix computation is made here.

Bradiant was developed as a deliberately small, Python-native, *table-first auditing layer*: its primary input is a complete numerical dataset, its principal result is an inspectable consistency report, and it can be used without adopting a category solver or a circuit simulator. Explicit charge labels, stored complex coefficients, ordered fusion paths, and JSON model fingerprints are intended to make discrepancies between independently produced datasets easier to reproduce. The separate implementation is motivated by interoperability and auditability, rather than claims of improved generality or execution speed.

# Software design

The software separates structural validation, fusion-tree transformations, coherence auditing, braid representations, and gauge-independent diagnostics. This decomposition prevents a numerical tolerance check from silently compensating for an incomplete fusion table. The model layer rejects missing allowed entries and incompatible charge structures; the tree layer enumerates deterministic bases and builds local recouplings; the audit layer composes independent paths and records sector-specific residuals. The braid layer moves an exchange to a local fusion vertex, applies the corresponding R symbol, and transforms the result back to the declared basis. It tests the Yang--Baxter relation and commutation of distant generators.

Two design constraints are important. First, deterministic basis conventions make the numerical output comparable across runs, whereas the individual matrix entries may change under vertex rephasings. Second, the software reports selected gauge-independent quantities separately: Frobenius--Perron dimensions are calculated from fusion matrices, and channel monodromy is obtained from opposite-order exchanges. This distinguishes table-dependent diagnostics from quantities that should be unchanged by an admissible gauge transformation. The implementation favors simple NumPy arrays and explicit enumeration over optimized sparse calculations, so the current intended use is for small finite models.

Bradiant assumes multiplicity-free, finite, unitary input and supports homogeneous external charges for its braid-group representations. It does not solve pentagon/hexagon equations, handle general fusion multiplicities or mixed-charge braid groupoids, certify exact algebraic identities, or predict device-level behavior. Numerical residuals near machine precision are evidence of consistency under a chosen tolerance, not mathematical proof.

# Research impact statement

The repository provides executable reference models and verification scripts that make its behavior testable without a hardware backend. Fibonacci and Ising examples exercise non-Abelian F/R data, while pointed cyclic examples expose transparent and nontransparent charges. The checked Fibonacci and Ising datasets produce maximum hexagon residuals of approximately $3.14\times10^{-16}$ and $3.41\times10^{-16}$, respectively, in double precision. The reference tests recover the Fibonacci quantum dimension $(1+\sqrt{5})/2$ and the Ising dimension $\sqrt{2}$; additional tests deliberately corrupt input data or rephase fusion vertices to test failure detection and gauge invariance. The repository includes a reproducible reference-output generator, a timing protocol, and continuous integration across supported Python versions.

This establishes reproducible numerical checks on concrete category datasets without claiming a new physical result. The source, tests, datasets, and command-line reproduction procedures are publicly available at [the Bradiant repository](https://github.com/qntmspark/Bradiant). The results reported here are internal software verification, not evidence of independent external adoption.
